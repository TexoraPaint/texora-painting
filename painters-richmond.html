Timing a paint job around Richmond's weather

Exterior paint needs a dry, warm surface to bond properly. Most exterior coatings call for a surface temperature above roughly 10°C with no rain in the forecast for several hours afterward, sometimes longer depending on the product. In a coastal climate, that generally makes spring through early fall the safer window for a full exterior repaint, while smaller touch-ups can often go ahead in shorter dry stretches at other times of year. Interior work is less weather-dependent: paint cures against an indoor climate you control, so opening a window or running a fan between coats is usually enough to keep things moving even in a wet stretch.

Richmond's climate is classified as oceanic. Because the city sits farther from the mountains than Vancouver, it receives roughly 30% less annual rainfall than its neighbour. Richmond's high groundwater table is also the reason very few homes in the city have basements. That combination, lower rainfall but water close to the surface, is worth keeping in mind at ground level: if paint or drywall near the bottom of an exterior wall looks different from the rest of the surface, whether that's bubbling, staining, or a chalky texture, it's worth having someone look at what's underneath before painting over it.

Questions worth asking before you hire a painter

A written estimate is the starting point. Ask for one before any work begins, and make sure it spells out the scope: which rooms or surfaces, how many coats, what prep is included, and what paint brand and sheen will be used. If a company won't put pricing and scope in writing, ask why before you sign anything.

It's also reasonable to ask whether a company carries liability insurance. Painters spend the day on ladders, moving furniture, and working close to your floors and finishes, and a straightforward yes or no answer tells you a lot about how the company operates.

Ask what the warranty actually covers, and get it in writing along with the estimate. A workmanship warranty should cover failures caused by the painter's own work, not normal wear, fading, or a problem that started somewhere else in the building.

  • Current condition of siding, stucco, or trim, including any caulking that has failed
  • Staining or discolouration near rooflines, window sills, or where a wall meets the ground
  • Whether the existing paint is chalking or peeling, or still sound enough to paint over directly
  • Access needs, like fencing, gates, or second-storey reach, that could affect scheduling

Repairs that come before a repaint

If a popcorn ceiling is part of what's holding a room back, that's usually its own step rather than something folded automatically into an interior repaint. Removing textured ceiling coating means scraping or skim-coating the surface smooth, sanding it, and then priming and painting it, and it's worth planning as a separate line item on your quote.

Soft, bubbling, or discoloured drywall usually points to moisture that got in somewhere: a leak, condensation, or a failed seal around a window or door. The standard fix is to cut out the damaged section, let the area dry out and get inspected, then fit, tape, and mud in new drywall over several thin coats before it's sanded smooth and primed. Finding where the moisture came from matters more than the patch itself. Painting over a repair without solving the leak behind it usually just means doing the same repair again later.

Texture matching is its own consideration when only part of a wall or ceiling needs patching rather than the whole surface. Drywall texture, whether it's a light orange peel, a heavier knockdown, or a smoother finish, has to be blended into the surrounding area before paint goes on, or the repair will show through as a flat patch once the light hits it. A patch that's been sanded and primed but not texture-matched can look worse under a bright window than the damage it replaced.
