Drywall Installation for Port Coquitlam Renovations & Developments

Port Coquitlam's family homes are some of the most heavily renovated in the Tri-Cities area. With large lots, split-level and two-storey layouts, and unfinished basements that represent thousands of square feet of untapped living space, PoCo homeowners are constantly developing, expanding, and updating. Drywall installation is at the centre of nearly every one of these projects — and getting it done right the first time is what separates a renovation that looks polished and professional from one that looks like a DIY project.

We work on all types of drywall installation projects in Port Coquitlam: full basement developments where the entire below-grade level is being converted to livable space, individual room additions where a garage or bonus room is being enclosed, feature walls and entertainment niches in main-floor renovations, and ceiling replacements where outdated popcorn or damaged drywall needs to be removed and replaced. Each scenario has its own board requirements, finishing demands, and code considerations — moisture-resistant board in bathrooms and laundry rooms, fire-rated Type X in utility and furnace rooms, standard board everywhere else.

If you want Level 5 (full skim coat) for a perfectly smooth wall under flat paint or wallpaper, we offer that as an upgrade. We can also paint the completed walls and ceilings as part of the same project — one crew, zero scheduling headaches.

Basement finishing and room additions: what actually happens

Drywall installation in a house usually means one of two things: a basement being turned into real living space, or a single room being added or reconfigured, like enclosing a garage bay or splitting an oversized bedroom. The two projects share a lot of the same steps, but the sequence and pace differ. A basement development touches every wall and the ceiling at once, so it's usually planned as one continuous push. A single room addition can often be squeezed around a family's normal week with less disruption.

Board type matters more than people think

Not every wall gets the same board, and getting this wrong is one of the more common corners cut on a rushed job. Bathrooms, laundry rooms, and any wall backing onto a below-grade concrete surface call for moisture-resistant board, since ordinary paper-faced drywall soaks up ambient dampness over time and can eventually soften or mould behind the paint. Furnace rooms and other spaces where code requires a fire separation need fire-rated Type X board, which has glass fibres mixed into the gypsum core to slow a fire's progress through the wall. Everywhere else, standard board does the job at a lower cost, so a room that mixes a bathroom, a furnace closet, and open living space usually ends up using two or three different board types under one roof.

What a finish level means for your paint job

The finishing level behind a wall decides how forgiving the paint on top of it will be. A Level 4 finish, three coats of compound over joints and screws, sanded smooth, is the standard for most rooms and holds up fine under an eggshell or satin paint in normal light. A Level 5 finish adds a thin skim coat over the entire surface, not just the joints, which is worth the extra step under flat paint, a light colour, or a wall that gets raking light from a window at a low angle, since any texture difference between the taped joints and the field of the board shows up under that kind of light. Glossier paint and stronger side lighting both make an underfinished wall more obvious, so it's worth matching the finish level to how the room will actually be lit and painted rather than defaulting to the cheaper option everywhere.

Living upstairs while the basement gets finished

A basement development usually runs over multiple weeks, and the household upstairs still has to function the whole time. It's worth asking how the noisy, dusty stages (cutting board, sanding compound) will be phased through the day rather than spread across it, how the stairwell opening will be sealed off (plastic sheeting and a zipper door are a common way to do it), and whether dust extraction is used on the sanders. The goal is that the rest of the house stays usable in the evenings and on weekends, even while the basement itself is mid-project. For room additions above grade, the same questions apply at a smaller scale: how dust will be contained to the room being worked in, and how often cleanup happens over a multi-day job.

What to ask for in an installation quote

A clear installation quote should break down the board type for each area, the finish level you're getting (Level 4 versus Level 5), whether painting is included or priced separately, and a realistic timeline with drying time built in between coats rather than compressed to look faster on paper. It's also worth asking who handles corner bead and outside corners specifically, since a sloppy outside corner is one of the more visible mistakes on an otherwise good drywall job.
