How elastomeric coatings work on stucco

Elastomeric paint is thicker than regular exterior latex, and it's formulated to stretch, often several hundred percent beyond its original size, and then return to shape as a stucco or masonry surface expands and contracts with temperature swings. It only works as intended, though, when it goes on at the right thickness.

Signs stucco needs more than a fresh coat

Hairline cracking is normal and exactly what a quality elastomeric system is built to handle. Cracks wider than roughly the thickness of a credit card, staining that keeps coming back in the same spot after cleaning, or sections that sound hollow when tapped are signs of a substrate problem rather than a paint problem, and no topcoat fixes those on its own.

Cure time for elastomeric coatings

Elastomeric coatings take longer to fully cure than standard latex paint, often two to four weeks before the film reaches full flexibility and hardness, even though it feels dry to the touch within a matter of hours. Rain or heavy dew hitting a section before that cure window closes can affect how well the coating bonds long-term, which is one more reason elastomeric work gets scheduled around a longer stretch of dry weather than a straightforward latex repaint would need.

Soffits and fascia on a repaint

Soffits and fascia boards sit close to gutters and are one of the first places moisture damage shows up, but they're also easy to skip on a repaint because they're not the surface anyone notices from the street. Checking for soft spots, peeling near the gutter line, and gaps where caulking has pulled away from the fascia board is worth doing every time an exterior gets painted.

Warranty terms on stucco work

The written estimate states the warranty period, one, two, or three years depending on scope, and it covers adhesion and coating failures traced back to prep or application. It doesn't cover cracking or moisture problems that trace back to the stucco itself rather than the paint sitting on top of it. Full terms are on the warranty page.

What to ask about access equipment for a two-storey exterior

Ladders alone aren't always enough to reach a gable end or a section above a sloped yard safely, and a contractor working without proper scaffolding or lift equipment on those sections is cutting a corner. It's a fair question to ask directly: what equipment gets used on the highest and hardest-to-reach sections of the house, and is the crew insured specifically for elevated work.

Testing colour on stucco before committing

A larger sample, brushed onto an actual section of siding or stucco and left up for a few days, shows how the colour reads in morning light, afternoon sun, and shade, which a small swatch never fully captures. This matters more on stucco than on smooth siding, since the texture itself changes how a colour reads compared to a flat paint chip.
